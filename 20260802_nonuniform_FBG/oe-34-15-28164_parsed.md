Abstract: We propose and experimentally demonstrate an optical microheater based on metal-
doped optical fibers (MDFs) that exploits the intrinsic 1480 nm absorption of Co2+ and Fe2+ /Fe3+
ions co-doped in an aluminosilicate core. Three MDFs with attenuation coefficients of 9.45,
12.58, and 32.43 dB/cm are fusion-spliced to single-mode fibers to form 10-mm-long heaters.
Under 367 mW pumping, the peak surface temperatures reach 166, 268, and 478 °C, while the
corresponding hot-zone lengths (FWHM) decrease from 10.6 to 2.8 mm, demonstrating that the
attenuation coefficient is a single design parameter that simultaneously controls both temperature
and spatial confinement. A Beer–Lambert distributed heat source coupled with a cylindrical
fin model predicts the peak temperatures of the remaining samples within 5–21% from a single
calibration point. Independent fiber Bragg grating measurements verify the thermographic results
within 18 °C, and optically induced carbonization of an acrylate recoating confirms the practical
heating capability. Unlike conventional coating-based fiber microheaters, the proposed device
requires no external photothermal layer, is directly fusion-spliceable to standard SMFs, and
enables attenuation-controlled tailoring of both the peak temperature and hot-zone length.

1.   Introduction
A microheater is a miniaturized heating device with dimensions of a few micrometers to several
hundred micrometers, fabricated using conductive thin-film resistor technology, microelectrome-
chanical systems (MEMS), or optical photothermal effects [1–4]. Owing to its ability to deliver
fast and precise localized heating with low input power, microheaters have been widely employed
in diverse fields, including responsivity enhancement of gas sensors, droplet ejection in inkjet
printer heads, sample heating in polymerase chain reaction (PCR) instruments for biochemical
analysis, and wearable thermal devices [5–8].
   Optical microheaters generate localized heat at a target position by remote delivery of optical
energy and are particularly suited to applications that are difficult to address with electrical
microheaters, such as temperature control in microfluidic systems, thermal tuning of photonic
devices, and precision thermal management in confined spaces [9–12]. Fiber-based architectures,
in particular, allow physical separation between the light source and the heating element, thereby
reducing wiring complexity, providing immunity to electromagnetic interference (EMI), and
enabling easy insertion or embedding into narrow and harsh environments [13,14]. These features
make fiber-optic microheaters a promising next-generation localized heat source platform that
complements the structural limitations of conventional electrical microheaters [15].
   Most fiber-optic microheaters reported to date rely on photothermal conversion layers—such
as metal thin films, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, or silicon—deposited on the fiber
tip [16–19]. However, these approaches require additional coating processes and are sensitive
to variations in coating thickness, uniformity, and adhesion, which makes reproducibility
difficult to ensure. Long-term operation also suffers from coating degradation and increased
packaging complexity, limiting their extension to high-reliability and high-durability microheater
applications.

   In this paper, we propose and experimentally analyze an optical microheater that exploits
the intrinsic optical absorption of metal-ion-doped silica fibers. Because the heating element
is formed by a single fusion splice between a metal-doped fiber (MDF) and a standard single-
mode fiber (SMF), the proposed structure fundamentally mitigates surface adhesion problems,
simplifies the device geometry, and is compatible with mass production. Three MDFs with
different optical attenuation characteristics are fabricated, and the peak surface temperature and
the spatial extent of the heated region (hot zone) are systematically analyzed under 1480 nm
photoexcitation using a high-performance thermal-imaging camera. The temperature distribution
is theoretically predicted by combining a Beer–Lambert distributed heat source with a cylindrical
fin heat-transfer model and is compared with experiment. The thermographic results are then
independently validated using a fiber Bragg grating (FBG) attached to the microheater surface,
and the practical heating capability is confirmed by optically induced thermal damage of an
acrylate polymer recoating. Therefore, the present approach provides a coating-free, fully
spliceable, and attenuation-engineered all-fiber microheater platform.

2.   Theory
The optical power propagating along a metal-doped fiber attenuates exponentially in the propaga-
tion direction (z-axis) according to the Beer–Lambert law [20]:

                                       P(z) = P0 exp(−αz),                                                (1)

where P0 is the input optical power and α is the absorption coefficient (Np/m). Experimentally
measured values in dB/cm are converted to Np/m by the standard unit conversion [20]. The
absorbed optical energy is assumed to be almost entirely converted into heat through non-radiative
relaxation processes within the metal-doped fiber [21]. The heat-generation rate per unit length
q(z) [W/m] at position z is therefore given by [22]

                                q(z) = α P(z) = α P0 exp(−αz).                                            (2)

   Equation (2) describes an exponentially decaying distributed heat source that is maximum at
the input end (z = 0) and minimum at the output end (z = L), and serves as the key input function
of the present model.
   The fiber, with a diameter d = 125 µm, is modeled as a thin cylindrical fin suspended in air
[23]. Under steady-state conditions, the energy balance over an infinitesimal element dz consists
of axial conduction, convective and radiative surface losses, and volumetric heat generation [23]:

                        −k A (d2 T/dz2 ) + heff π d (T − Tamb ) = q(z),                                   (3)

where T(z) denotes the local fiber surface temperature, Tamb is the ambient temperature, k = 1.38
W/(m·K) is the thermal conductivity of silica [24], A = π(d/2)2 is the cross-sectional area, and heff
is the effective heat-transfer coefficient that accounts for both convection and radiation [25,26].
   The characteristic decay length of axial conduction is given by [27]
                                            √︂
                                      ℓ = k A / ( heff π d ).                                    (4)

   Substituting the experimentally calibrated value of heff (Section 3.4) yields ℓ ≈ 0.17 mm, which
is more than two orders of magnitude smaller than the sample length L = 10 mm. Therefore,
except for a region of width ±ℓ near the splice, the axial conduction term can be neglected and

3.     Experimental results and discussion
3.1.    Optical properties of the metal-doped fibers
Three types of MDFs (MDF01, MDF02, MDF03) were used in this study. The fibers were
fabricated by combining the modified chemical vapor deposition (MCVD) process with a
solution-doping technique. For solution doping, the porous silica soot layer deposited inside the
substrate tube by MCVD was immersed in an ethanol-based precursor solution. Iron(III) chloride
hexahydrate (FeCl3 ·6H2 O) and cobalt(II) chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2 ·6H2 O) were used as the
Fe and Co precursors, respectively, and were dissolved in ethanol for 2 h. The soot layer was
soaked in the precursor solution for 1 h to impregnate the metal ions; the residual solution was
then removed, and the tube was dried, sintered, and collapsed to form the doped-core preform.
The core refractive index was tailored not by GeCl4 but by AlCl3 , which is added during solution
doping to suppress aggregation of transition-metal ions [29]. Cobalt (Co) and iron (Fe) were
incorporated into each core, and the optical absorption originates predominantly from Co2+
and Fe2+ /Fe3+ ions. Co2+ in tetrahedral coordination produces a broad absorption band over
1300–1550 nm, whereas Fe3+ contributes mainly to losses in the visible region [30]. Hence, at the
1480 nm pump wavelength used in this work, heat generation is expected to arise predominantly
from Co2+ absorption. Aluminum (Al) does not introduce any effective absorption band from
the visible to the infrared and therefore does not directly contribute to optical absorption [31].
   The elemental composition of each fiber core was analyzed by field-emission scanning
electron microscopy (FE-SEM, SU8600, Hitachi Inc.) equipped with an energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector. Cross-sectional samples were prepared by mounting the
fibers vertically in epoxy, curing overnight, and polishing the end face; the core center was
identified in the FE-SEM image, and EDS quantification was performed at that position. Table 1
lists the full EDS compositions, including O, Si, Al, Fe, and Co. The measured compositions
confirm that MDF03 has the highest Co and Fe contents among the three fibers. The relative
photothermal contributions of Co and Fe can be quantified from the molar absorptivities of the
two ions in vitreous silica [30]. At 1480 nm, the molar absorptivity of Co is approximately
14 cm−1 /(mol·L−1 ), about 5.4 times that of Fe (approximately 2.6 cm−1 /(mol·L−1 )). Combining
this ratio with the molar dopant ratios obtained from the EDS analysis (Table 1), Co accounts for
essentially all of the absorption in the Fe-free MDF01 and for approximately 88% and 94% of
the 1480 nm absorption in MDF02 and MDF03, respectively. Thus, although both ions absorb in
the near-infrared, Co2+ is the dominant photothermal contributor at the pump wavelength in all
three fibers.

3.4.     Validation of the temperature-prediction model
The Beer–Lambert distributed heat source–cylindrical fin model proposed in Section 2 was
applied to the experimental data. Using the measured peak surface temperature of MDF01
(9.45 dB/cm) at 367 mW (Tmax = 166 °C, Tamb = 23 °C), the effective heat-transfer coefficient
was calibrated via Eq. (6) to be heff ≈ 1,451 W/(m2 ·K). The corresponding axial conduction
decay length, calculated from Eq. (4), is ℓ ≈ 0.17 mm—more than two orders of magnitude
smaller than L = 10 mm—confirming the validity of the local-equilibrium assumption.
   Using the calibrated heff , the predicted maximum surface temperatures of MDF02 (12.58 dB/cm)
and MDF03 (32.43 dB/cm) at the same input power are approximately 210 °C and 501 °C,
respectively. Compared with the measured values of 268 °C and 478 °C, the prediction errors are
approximately 21% [= (268−210)/268] for MDF02 and 5% [= (501−478)/478] for MDF03. The
larger discrepancy for MDF02 than for MDF03 is mainly attributed to the simplified structure
of the model and the intermediate heating regime of MDF02. In the model, the predicted peak
temperature approximately linearly with the input-end attenuation [Eq. (6)], so any longitudinal
non-uniformity in the dopant or absorption profile can strongly affect the effective near-input
heat generation. In addition, MDF02 operates in a transitional regime: its hot-zone length
increases with pump power [Fig. 4(b)], whereas MDF01 and MDF03 remain nearly constant. The
single-heff local-equilibrium model is therefore most representative for the two limiting cases of
distributed heating (MDF01) and strongly localized heating (MDF03), and less accurate for the
intermediate MDF02 case. The model underprediction for MDF02 (210 vs. 268 °C) suggests that
its actual near-input absorption may be higher than the length-averaged attenuation value. Even
so, the calibrated heff obtained from a single reference point captures both the qualitative and the
quantitative thermal behaviors of samples with different attenuation coefficients, demonstrating
the predictive capability of the proposed model and the consistency of the thermographic
measurements.

an apparent peak temperature of approximately 460 °C when the temperature-dependent FBG

4.   Conclusion
We have fabricated three 1-cm-long MDF microheaters with different optical attenuation
characteristics and have systematically evaluated their thermal characteristics under 1480 nm
photoexcitation. The measured attenuation coefficients were 9.45, 12.58, and 32.43 dB/cm
for MDF01, MDF02, and MDF03, respectively. Thermographic measurements and axial
temperature-profile analysis confirmed that the heating characteristics depend strongly on the
attenuation coefficient.
   As the attenuation increased, the peak surface temperature rose markedly while the hot-zone
length decreased. At the maximum input power of 367 mW, the average peak surface temperatures
reached approximately 166, 268, and 478 °C for MDF01, MDF02, and MDF03, respectively,
with corresponding hot-zone lengths of approximately 10.6, 8.9, and 2.8 mm. These results
demonstrate that the attenuation coefficient of the MDF is an effective design parameter that allows
distributed and localized heating regimes to be addressed independently within an essentially
identical device geometry.
   The Beer–Lambert distributed heat source–cylindrical fin model predicted the peak tempera-
tures of the remaining samples from a single calibration point with errors of approximately 5%
for MDF03 and 21% for MDF02. To verify the reliability of the thermographic measurements, an
independent FBG-based temperature measurement was carried out on MDF03; at the maximum
input power of 367 mW, the FBG-deduced peak temperature was approximately 460 °C, differing
by only ≈18 °C from the 478 °C measured by the thermal-imaging camera. In addition, optically
induced thermal decomposition and carbonization of an acrylate polymer recoating directly
demonstrated the practical heating capability of the device.
   In summary, this work demonstrates that the heating temperature and the spatial confinement
of the hot zone of an optical microheater can be controlled simultaneously by tailoring the
attenuation of a metal-doped optical fiber that is fusion-spliced to standard SMFs. The proposed
structure benefits from a simple fabrication process, excellent thermal stability and reliability,
and seamless integration with conventional fiber-optic systems. Future work will focus on
optimizing the metal-doping concentration, tailoring the active length, selecting the excitation
wavelength, and implementing closed-loop temperature control to extend the device to higher
operating temperatures and a broader range of thermal functions. We believe that the proposed
MDF microheater offers a versatile and scalable platform for thermal ablation, localized polymer
